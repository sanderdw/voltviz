VoltViz Music Visualizer

The audio engine, explained

How VoltViz hears the beat

One engine listens to the music for every visualizer. It detects onsets, estimates the tempo and keeps a beat grid. A beat fires only when a kick or snare is heard on that grid, about 20 ms after the hit: nothing is predicted, and when the drums stop the beats stop. The signal path

One engine feeds every visualizer

A session has one AudioContext. The source splits in two. The analysis path goes straight into an AudioWorklet on the audio thread, which finds onsets, tempo and beats. The display path goes through the Auto Gain stage into shared analysers that provide the spectra and waveforms visualizers draw.

Once per animation frame the engine packs everything into one AudioFrame. Every visualizer reads that frame, and none of them touch Web Audio. Before the rewrite, each visualizer built its own audio graph and guessed the beat in its own way.

Dots travel at the pace of the simulation: blue pulses are fired beats, violet ones are AI decisions. The Auto Gain wire lights up when the switch is on.

Stage 1 · every 5.8 ms

Onsets: what just started to sound

Every 5.8 ms the worklet takes a new hop of samples, runs a 1024-point FFT and splits the spectrum into quarter-octave bands. An onset is a sudden rise in energy, so the engine measures how much each band grew compared with 11.6 ms earlier. It measures on a log scale, which makes the result the same for quiet and loud input.

Three onset functions come out: kick (35–150 Hz, measured on the waveform because an FFT this size has only three bins down there), mid (150 Hz–6 kHz: kick clicks, claps, snares) and hat (6 kHz and up). A peak counts when it tops the median of the last half second by 1.5 to 2 mean deviations, so the threshold follows the music.

Measured on the mix · which band points at the beat?

Stretches of 16 beats in which a comb over that band's onsets chose the right beat phase, on the first 2-minute excerpt of the mix.

The mix is full of traps. Rolling basslines fill the gaps between kicks, a pickup lands just before the beat, and open hats sit on the off-beat. Measured on the real audio, only the mid band pointed at the beat every time.

So the phase of the beat grid comes from the mid band alone, and the tempo estimate also uses the broadband flux. Beats themselves fire on kick and snare onsets, but only on those that land on the grid.

Stage 2 · four times a second

Tempo: how often does it repeat?

Four times a second the engine compares the last 8 seconds of onsets with delayed copies of themselves (an autocorrelation). A delay that lines the onsets up again is a candidate beat period.

A candidate earns extra support when its 2×, 3× and 4× multiples line up too, and when something happens halfway between its beats. Dance music is ambiguous between 65, 130 and 260 BPM, so a gentle prior around 128 BPM breaks the tie. Other music has other habits: the Music style setting moves the prior, e.g. to 85 BPM for ballads or 170 BPM for hardcore.

A beat period must also repeat by itself: half-time drums with hits on every other beat only are tracked at the rate of the hits. Jumps to a 3:2 or 4:3 relative of a tempo followed with confidence need much stronger evidence, because dotted and syncopated rhythms repeat at exactly those ratios.

Stage 3 · the beat grid

A beat is a hit you heard, on the grid

The engine keeps a beat grid: a clock that runs at the tempo and checks the onsets around each of its beats, moving its phase a quarter of the error (a phase-locked loop). Four times a second a comb over the last 32 beats scores every possible phase. When the comb keeps disagreeing with the grid, the grid re-locks. A jump of half a beat, onto the off-beat, needs twice as much evidence, because off-beat hats and basslines are the classic trap.

The grid never fires anything itself. A beat fires when a kick or snare is heard within 50 ms of a grid beat, while the tempo is confident. beat.isBeat is true in the first frame after the analysis detected that hit, about 20 ms after it sounded. Hits between the beats, such as the pickup, a bassline note or an off-beat hat, are heard but ignored. When the drums stop, nothing fires: the engine doesn't make up beats to fill a breakdown. A hit also has to be at least 15% as strong as the recent beats, so faint onsets in a pad or a riser don't count.

Beat strength says how hard the beats hit. The engine lines up the level of the kick band, the snare band and the whole signal around the last beats and measures how far it jumps on the beat. A full kick gives about 1, a build-up over a pumping pad or a soft thud a small value. Visualizers scale their beat effects by it, so a build-up pulses gently and the drop hits hard.

A new song resets the grid's caution. The engine notices one after half a second of silence, or when the sound and the tempo change together (a DJ mix keeps its tempo and is left alone); a Sendspin track change counts too. The comb and the tempo estimate then listen to the new song only, and the grid takes the new phase as soon as six beats of it are clear.

Stage 4 · Settings → Music style

Which pulse do the effects follow?

The tempo says how fast the grid runs; the Music style decides what the effects follow on it. Most music flashes on every beat. Dubstep, trap and a lot of drum & bass are written at 140 or 174 BPM but felt at half: a kick on 1, a snare on 3. With the Dubstep, drum & bass, trap style the engine keeps tracking the steadier 140 BPM grid and fires every other beat, the ones with the kick and the snare.

Auto makes no genre assumption. It keeps the dance-music prior and only goes half-time on unmistakable evidence: for about seven seconds nothing, and no kick, on every other beat. A DJ's half-time build-up therefore still flashes on every beat. When the effects run at double or half the speed you feel, pick a style; Settings shows what the engine follows right now.

Stage 5 · Settings → AI Beat Tracking

A neural network decides which beat

The DSP grid keeps time within milliseconds, but it can settle confidently on the wrong element, such as the off-beat hats. Then the kicks fall outside its windows and the beats stop, or land on off-beat sounds. The network knows which element is the beat.

Every 5 seconds (every 2.5 s while it settles in) the worklet sends the last 10 seconds of audio, as a 128-band log-mel spectrogram at 50 frames per second, to beat_this small0 (2.1 M parameters, MIT licence). The model runs in a Web Worker, on your device. The arbiter compares the beats it finds with the grid:

Same phase
confirm, nudge ½ if > 15 ms
Off by ≥ 13% of a beat
shift the phase (two windows agree if the previous one confirmed it)
Double, half or 3:2 tempo
retime (two windows agree)
4:3 tempo, or clock lost
retime, only if the DSP's own estimate no longer backs its clock

12 s: no phase jump · 30 s: no octave, 3:2 or 4:3 tempo jump
Downbeats
which grid beat is the “1” of the bar; a change needs two windows

It is off by default, in the app and on this page: the grid is left to the DSP comb, which in this groove falls onto the off-beat at the drop and only finds its way back after several bars. Switch it on to let the network put the grid back on the beat. In the app, off stops the model's worker, so the 9.9 MB model is not loaded and costs no CPU. If the model can't run, the engine falls back to DSP-only tracking.

Stage 6 · Settings → Auto Gain

Auto Gain: loud looks from a quiet mic

Visualizers draw the byte spectrum of an AnalyserNode, which maps −100…−30 dBFS onto 0…255. A microphone across the room delivers much less level than system audio, so the same song draws smaller bars.

With Auto Gain on, the engine tracks the loudness of the input and turns a gain stage in front of the display analysers towards a target of −14 dBFS. When the music gets louder the gain comes down within about half a second; when it gets quieter the gain climbs back over several seconds, so breakdowns keep their shape. During silence the gain is frozen and never amplifies the noise floor.

Beat detection sits before the gain and works on a log scale, so the switch never changes where beats fire. It is off by default, so every visualizer looks exactly as it was tuned until you turn it on.

Stage 7 · once per animation frame

What a visualizer receives

A visualizer is a small module with a frame() function. It gets the same AudioFrame as every other layer, including both layers during a crossfade.

// fire on the beat, as hard as it hits; glide on the phase
if (audio.beat.isBeat) flash = audio.beat.strength;
flash *= 0.9;
ring.rotation = audio.beat.phase * Math.PI * 2;
kickPulse = audio.onsets.kick.envelope;
bars = audio.spectrum({ fftSize: 2048 });

isBeat is true in the first frame after a beat of the pulse was heard, and strength (0–1) says how hard it hit. phase runs from 0 at the last heard beat to 1 one beat later, and stays at 1 when the beats stop. On a half-time pulse bpm is the pulse (70) and tempo the tracked tempo (140). Onset envelopes decay with a 150 ms time constant. gain reports what Auto Gain applies. With AI Beat Tracking on, barBeat counts from the network's downbeats (barKnown) and downbeat marks the “1”.
