The project history in one line. It started on 15 March 2026 as a Google AI Studio app; the Gemini SDK was removed on day one. Nine days later it was public with over thirty visualizers. Music Assistant support via Sendspin came in April, skins and the Audio Debug view in May, and by the end of August there were 54 visualizers and a Home Assistant app. 78 commits, 23 releases. The rewrite this talk is about happened on 27 September, in one AI session.

Chapter 1 · The debt

Every visualizer was its own island

Each new visualizer arrived as a self-contained component — with its own audio graph, its own analysers and its own idea of what a beat is.

Perfect for shipping fast. The repository even carried an AI-agent recipe for adding visualizers that prescribed exactly this pattern.

56

components, each with its own AudioContext

2

audio contexts running during every crossfade

0

tempo trackers — no notion of the beat grid

The pattern that made VoltViz grow fast is also what made it fragile. Every one of the 56 visualizer components created its own AudioContext and analysers, so during a crossfade two audio graphs ran at once. Nothing tracked the tempo. The repo's own AI-agent recipe for adding a visualizer told the agent to do exactly this: set up your own audio, close it on unmount.

Over-reactive visualizers were tamed by hand — the changelog literally reads “reduced sensitivity by a 0.8× damping factor”.

A survey agent read all 56 files and found six families of beat detection, from a reasonable spectral-flux detector to "the bass is above 180 and went up by ten". None of them tracked the tempo, and each was tuned by hand for its visualizer. On the mix, as we will see, the best of them reaches a beat F-measure of 0.58.

Chapter 1 · The brief — one prompt

“Rewrite the complete architecture. Separate the audio engine from the visualizations. Keep all end-user functionality.”

“The most important quality rule: check that visualizations really work on a real DJ mix. Make sure beat detection is really working properly.”

+ an HTML report as evidence, in the repository · the owner's prompt, lightly edited

This was the entire brief, one prompt: three goals and one quality rule. The quality rule is the interesting part for this audience: it does not ask for code, it asks for proof, on a real DJ mix, that the beat detection really works. Everything in chapter two follows from taking that rule seriously.

Chapter 2 · Architecture

One engine, one analysis, one frame

Before

56 × ( AudioContext → analysers → own beat guess → drawing )

Audio analysis ran at the render frame rate, inside React components, and twice during every crossfade.

After

Mic · system audio · Sendspin

AudioWorklet (audio thread)

onsets · tempo · predictive beat clock

Neural Worker

beat model picks which beat

One AudioFrame per frame: beat · onsets · bands · spectra

VisualizerHost: one render loop → 56 renderer modules

Before: every visualizer owned a full audio pipeline and analysed at the render frame rate. After: sources feed one engine. The analysis runs in an AudioWorklet on the audio thread at a fixed 5.8 millisecond hop, independent of the frame rate; a small neural model runs in a Web Worker. Visualizers become plain modules that receive one shared AudioFrame per animation frame and never touch Web Audio.

Chapter 2 · Ground truth

First, decide what “correct” means

Two independent trackers — madmom (RNN + DBN) and beat_this (transformer, 2024) — agree within 0–10 ms.

Windows only count where both agree; the rest is reported as ambiguous.

librosa was rejected: it lost the beat after 30 seconds.

13 two-minute excerpts from across the mix.

Every kick's pitch drop starts on a reference beat (red lines).

Before writing any engine code, the AI established what correct means. Two independent state-of-the-art offline trackers with unrelated architectures, madmom and beat_this, produced the reference beats; they agree within 0 to 10 milliseconds. librosa was tried as a cross-check and rejected because it lost the beat. The AI also checked the reference physically: on the spectrogram every kick's pitch drop starts on a reference beat.

Chapter 2 · Listening with its eyes

It looked at the signal — and found the trap

Dashed magenta: an attack 75 ms early · red: the reference beat

A hit 75 ms before every other beat

Rolling basslines fill the off-beats and open hats sit on them. Bass onsets pointed early, treble onsets half a beat off.

So the engine takes its beat phase from 150 Hz – 6 kHz.

This is the moment I find most interesting. The first engine locked 75 to 90 milliseconds early, confidently. Instead of turning knobs, the AI rendered the waveform and spectrogram around individual beats and looked at them. It found a sharp low-frequency attack 75 milliseconds before every other beat, a pickup, followed by the real beat. Low-frequency onset detectors are fooled by it, and the band above 6 kHz is fooled by off-beat hats. That is why the engine takes its phase from the mid band.

This is the part I would ask every team using AI for engineering to copy. Each time a design was frozen, it was evaluated once on excerpts it had never heard. Twice it failed badly: the re-tuned DSP engine scored 0.15 and 0.32 on two fresh excerpts, locked onto the off-beat. That failure is what led to the hybrid. Excerpts that influenced a change were moved to the development set, and a new final set was drawn. The failures are in the report, not hidden.

Chapter 2 · The hybrid engine

Two experts, two jobs

Knows when

DSP beat clock

  • Audio thread, every 5.8 ms
  • Predicts the next beat, so effects land when it is heard
  • Phase-locked loop keeps timing within milliseconds

Knows which

Neural arbiter

  • beat_this small0: 2.1 M parameters, MIT
  • Every ~5 s on the last 10 s, in a Web Worker
  • Confirms, shifts the phase or fixes an octave

The network chooses which beat; the DSP clock decides when.

The final engine splits the problem. The DSP clock runs on the audio thread, predicts beats and keeps timing within milliseconds; it is fast but can be fooled about which of several consistent events is the beat. A 2.1 million parameter model, beat_this small0, MIT licensed, runs every five seconds on the last ten seconds in a Web Worker and only arbitrates the metrical phase: confirm, shift, or fix an octave error. It never sets the timing itself. The human made this call when the DSP-only design failed, after the AI laid out the trade-offs; it sits behind a Settings toggle, off by default so slow devices don't pay for it.

13 of 13 excerpts pass every gate: F ≥ 0.80, continuity ≥ 0.90, tempo right in ≥ 90% of windows, timing within 20 ms.

F-measure: share of beats found within ±70 ms (1.0 = all). AI Beat Tracking off (the default, DSP only): 0.818.

The numbers. Over all thirteen excerpts the new engine finds the beat with an F-measure of 0.979, and 0.974 on the four excerpts that were only ever evaluated once, after the design was frozen. The best of the six old detectors reaches 0.584 on the same audio. In the running app, beats fire a median of 4 milliseconds from the reference. Every excerpt passes every gate. Without the neural model the engine still reaches 0.818, and that is what users get out of the box: AI Beat Tracking is off by default.

Chapter 2 · Proof in the running app

Code that detects beats is not the goal; visuals that move on the beat are. A probe inside the app reads every rendered frame and measures whether the picture changes with the beat grid, compared with randomly jittered grids. 42 of 50 visualizers pass; the others render correctly but react in ways a short measurement cannot resolve, and the old app behaves the same for them. Measured the same way, the old detectors fired 80 to 100 milliseconds before the beat, on the pickup; the new engine's effects follow the beat. Auto Gain adds 16 dB for a quiet microphone.

Chapter 2 · Mistakes

It made mistakes — and measured its way out

MistakeHow it surfacedFix
Analysed the full mix at onceThe machine ran out of memoryMemory caps, 2-min excerpts
Tuned on audio it had already seenFresh excerpts: F 0.15Held-out sets, neural arbiter
Ran heavy jobs side by sideThe human saw “memory nearly full”Strictly sequential + watchdog
Test harness left pages open on timeoutsMemory kept climbingPages always closed, runs redone
Faster start-up weakened a safety ruleA tempo glitch at 30:00: F 0.88Windows ≥ 4.5 s apart: F 0.94

None of these were hidden: each one is in the report or the commit history.

It was not flawless, and I think that is the honest part of this story. It crashed the machine on the first real step. It tuned on audio it had already seen until fresh excerpts exposed it. It ran jobs side by side until the human saw a memory warning. Its own test harness leaked browser pages on timeouts, which showed up as climbing memory. And a latency improvement quietly weakened a safety rule. Each was caught by a measurement or by the human, fixed, and documented.

Chapter 2 · Lessons

What made the AI an expert, not a guesser

01

Independent ground truth

Two unrelated trackers decide what correct is — never the system under test

02

Held-out evaluation

Fresh audio after every design freeze; first-run failures stay on the record

03

Look at the signal

A spectrogram found the pickup that parameter tweaking never would

04

Measure the product

Rendered frames, old vs new, frame rates and memory — not only the model

If I had to boil it down to four habits: an independent ground truth, so the system never grades itself; held-out evaluation, with failures kept on the record; actually looking at the signal, which is where the pickup was found; and measuring the product people see, rendered frames and not just model scores. None of these are new. What is new is that an AI applied them on its own initiative, under a one-line quality rule, in one afternoon.
